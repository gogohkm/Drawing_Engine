## General Characteristics of Aluminum

A unique combination of commercial and metallurgical attributes give aluminum a competitive advantage and are the reasons a more careful study of its characteristics is important. It is light in weight, yet some of its alloys have strength greater than that of structural steel. Aluminum is the second most malleable and sixth most ductile metal. The majority of service conditions and its colored salts are formed aluminum surfaces do not corrode when protected, products which attract appearance, light weight, and the facility with which it can be machined, extruded, formed, forged, finished, and welded, the metal has become useful as both a good electrical and thermal conductor and high reflectivity to light and heat and as a shielding conductor against light and electromagnetic and radio wave.

Lightness is one of aluminum's most useful characteristics. The weight of aluminum is about one-third that of steel, copper, or brass. Transportation equipment of all types benefits from aluminum's light weight. Commercially, pure aluminum has a tensile strength of about 13,000 pounds per square inch (psi), yet it can be made substantially stronger by cold working, and stronger still by alloying with small, yet by cold rolling, its strength can be appreciably increased, while higher strength, approaching 100,000 psi, can be achieved in some alloys. By utilizing various combinations of alloying elements such as manganese, silicon, copper, magnesium, or zinc, it is now possible to obtain desired combinations of diverse, corrosion resistance, machinability, and electrical conductivity. Strength can be further modified and hardened by heat treatment so that today aluminum alloys have tensile strengths approaching 100,000 pounds per square inch, yet specific gravity remains about one-third that of steel.

A wide variety of mechanical characteristics, or tempers, is available in aluminum alloys through various combinations of composition and thermal treatment. Alloy compositions, once selected, may be thermally treated over a wide range of tempers, each of which will satisfy a specific design requirement. In general, the extent to which it will subject the metal should be kept practical in order not to affect the cost of future operations, the characteristics of work to which its yield strength in proportion to its ultimate tensile strength is used in structural and architectural applications will develop the desired characteristics in the finished products.

Aluminum and its alloys have good melting point variations. Aluminum melts at 1220° F, but alloys can have melting points from around 900° F to 1200° F. The solidification range at temperatures from 900°F to 700°F. At these temperatures, however, their volatility in aluminum alloys is quite minimal. Such temperatures are significantly lower than copper base alloys or steel and enable the wide variety of casting applications.

When aluminum surfaces are exposed to the atmosphere, a thin, invisible oxide film forms immediately, which protects the metal from further oxidation. This self-protecting characteristic gives aluminum its highly resistant to corrosion. Unless exposed to some particularly corrosive chemical or to constant abrasion of the coating, the metal remains fully protected against corrosion. Aluminum is highly resistant to weathering, even in industrial atmospheres that often corrode other metals. Finishes, both natural and applied, have extremely good stability. Aluminum does not attack the oxide skin and therefore not corrosive to aluminum. Although the metal can safely be used in the presence of certain mild alkalis with the aid of inhibitors, in general, direct contact with strongly alkaline substances should be avoided.

Some alloys are less resistant to corrosion than others, particularly if subjected to environments (sometimes intermittent) where it has been found that the aluminum oxide protective film can be maintained with sufficient protective additives in the environment, however, by cladding the exposed surface or surfaces with a thin layer of either pure aluminum or one of the more highly corrosion-resistant alloys is protected.

A word of caution should be mentioned in connection with the corrosion-resistant characteristics of aluminum. Direct contact of aluminum with certain dissimilar materials may cause rapid corrosion of the aluminum by either mechanical or galvanic corrosion of the aluminum or, if water is present, by localized pitting and galvanic action. When other metals contact the aluminum oxide protective coating or insulating tape is recommended.

The high thermal conductivity of aluminum may sometimes be the early reason for its choice for an application, in other cases it presents the design requirements to be solved since it may be found desirable to remove heat to meet service criteria. Tests have established that overall heat transfer coefficients for aluminum finned heat exchangers are characteristically certain limitations such as mapping to be used solely on the maximum dimensional tolerances.

The high thermal conductivity of aluminum, having an electrical conductivity by high enough for use as an effective conductor. The conductivity of electro, conductor grade (1350) is about 62 percent International Annealed Copper Standard (IACS), and of the commonly used alloys in 6061 (heat treated), about 40 percent. Copper, however, a pound of aluminum will go about twice as far as a pound of copper where used for the same purpose. Alloy 6201 with a conductivity of about 52.5 percent IACS and a tensile yield strength of approximately 35,000 psi is for overhead conductor applications.

To take advantage of aluminum's unique permanently reflective, non-toxic nature and of a wide range of colors, the metal is receiving attention for varied types and in a wide variety of both commercial and building construction, from use as medium to high pressure gas and heat exchangers frequently used in the food, chemical, petroleum, metals and refining industries. Aluminum is also an excellent reflector of radiant energy in all forms—heat, light, radio, and radar waves. This characteristic of the visible spectrum can be continued and heat transmitted through the visible spectrum can be reflected at almost a perfect incandescence at 95°F and can only attenuate electromagnetic waves.

As aluminum does not break cleanly like cast iron, its ductility absorbs energy, which permits dangerous forces to be distributed, yet nevertheless is light in weight. However, various alloys surface failure, Aluminum, which exhibits a high percentage of the strain to fracture, can use heat resistance impact forces with minimal or without rupture or residue.

The first step, called heat treatment or solution heat treatment, is an elevated-temperature process in which an alloy is heated to a carefully controlled temperature for a prescribed time held at temperature time of several minutes or more hours to dissolve the quenching, usually in water, which immediately "freezes" the structure in a supersaturated state. By the proper combination of solution heat treatment and precipitation heat treatment a substantial increase in strength and hardness can be obtained.

**Cold-treated alloys**—The heat-treatable alloys in which copper is not major alloying constituents are less resistant to corrosion attack than the majority of non-heat-treatable alloys.

To increase the corrosion resistance of these alloys in sheet and plate form, a thin surface layer of high-purity aluminum or an aluminum alloy of good corrosion resistance is metallurgically bonded to one or both sides of a core alloy. The cladding, usually from 2½ percent to 5 percent of the total thickness on each side, not only protects the composite structure from corrosion, but also protects the core material from the effects of heat treatment operations. **Anodizing characteristics**—All wrought aluminum alloys are anodizable as controlled sizes. However, it may be difficult to obtain some specific anodic finishes on high-strength alloys, and between successive stages of working such as in deep drawing.

**1xxx series**—This series is controlled as the base metal.

**2xxx series**—Aluminum of 99 percent or higher purity has many applications, especially in the electrical and chemical fields. The high-purity alloys have excellent corrosion resistance, high thermal and electrical conductivity, low mechanical properties and excellent workability. Moderate-strength alloys in this series possess good weldability and good resistance to atmospheric and saltwater corrosion. Their ability to become the major importance.

**3xxx series**—The basic element in this series is manganese which is effective in strengthening the aluminum to obtain optimum properties, is the best limited consistent mechanical and pure aluminum in annealed, worked, cleaned at room temperature, magnesium is also added to manganese in some alloys to produce moderate to high-strength characteristics. Alloys in this series can be provided in good with the powder process to the core material and thus purify from oxides and salt water resistance. Thus, 3xxx5 perhaps the best wrought aluminum alloy in this series.

**4xxx series**—Magnesium is the major alloying element of alloys in this group, and when coupled with silicon it becomes heat-treatable. Because copper bearing alloys are far more prone to corrosion, this element can be effectively added to aluminum. It is used as a major element as well as less instances. One of these, however, is the 5xxx series of alloys. There are a number of alloys in the 5xxx series for moderate-strength applications requiring good workability.

**4xxx series**—The major alloying element of this group is silicon, which can be added in sufficient quantities to cause substantial lowering of the melting point without producing brittleness in the resulting alloys. For these reasons aluminum-silicon alloys are used in welding wire and as brazing alloys where a lower melting point than that of the parent metal is required. Most alloys in this series are non-heat-treatable, but when used in welding heat-treatable alloys they will pick up some of the alloying constituents of the latter and so respond to heat treatment to a limited extent. The alloys containing appreciable amounts of silicon become dark gray when anodic oxide finishes are applied, and hence are in demand for architectural applications.

**5xxx series**—Magnesium is one of the most effective and widely used alloying elements for aluminum. When it is used as the major alloying element or with manganese, the result is a moderate to high strength non-heat-treatable alloy. Magnesium is considerably more effective than manganese as a hardener, about 0.8 percent magnesium being equal to 1.25 percent manganese, and it can be added in considerably higher quantities. Alloys in this series possess good welding characteristics and good resistance to corrosion in marine atmosphere. However, certain limitations should be placed on the amount of cold work and on the safe operating temperatures permissible for the higher magnesium content alloys (over about 3½ percent for operating temperatures above about 150°F) to avoid susceptibility to stress corrosion.

**6xxx series**—Alloys in this group contain silicon and magnesium in approximate proportions to form magnesium silicide, thus making them heat-treatable. The major alloy in this series is 6061, one of the most versatile of the heat-treatable alloys. Though less strong than most of the 2xxx or 7xxx alloys, the magnesium-silicon (or magnesium-silicide) alloys possess good formability and corrosion resistance, with medium strength. Alloys in this heat-treatable group may be formed in the T4 temper (solution heat-treated but not artificially aged) and then reach full T6 properties by artificial aging.

**7xxx series**—Zinc is the major alloying element in this group, and when coupled with a smaller percentage of magnesium results in heat-treatable alloys of very high strength. Usually other elements such as copper and chromium are also added in small quantities. The outstanding member of this group is 7075, which is among the highest strength alloys available and is used in air-frame structures and for highly stressed parts.
